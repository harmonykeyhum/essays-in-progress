LA ROBOTS

BY MELISSA McCARTER • PACIFIC TIME POCKET ANALYSIS

ATMOSPHERE: TANNED & RELAXED
AUTONOMY INDEX: LEVEL 4 HIGH
FOOD SENSORY: CONGEALED CHEESE
ESCAPE POD: BUR → STL

The Waymos are what surprise us first. On screen, they burn but in person, they blend. Maybe that is the motto of LA. My daughter says the natives are robots. Tanned, relaxed, standing in line at Starbucks like they have nothing else to do. We are at the airport. Not the planned one. LAX's flights were grounded because of the longest shutdown in the United States. So we walk through Burbank. Terra Cotta floors--Spanish 1920s. Flashes of Scripps College, 30 years ago. Maybe some places are pockets of time, holding memories like cards in a poker game.

The Uber driver points out the DoorDash robots. Up and down sidewalks. He is called to fix them when people can't figure out how to open them. My friend is incredulous when I told him. "Don't the cracks in the sidewalk from earthquakes make them fall?"

My tween daughter is convinced Waymos and DoorDash robots will go rogue. Megans, she says. Programmed to benefit humanity. "In Missouri, they would use those robots for target practice."

We can't figure out how to open the doors of more than one Uber car. They are nicer than other cars we have ever been in. The drivers have thick Russian accents. In the backseat, unsure of the etiquette, I ask ChatGPT if we are supposed to talk to them. "That is your choice but many drivers appreciate a quiet ride after a long day," ChatGPT says my tip is generous. The AI and I get into a discussion of how little Uber drivers make in LA. Million-dollar houses and Cadillacs. I imagine 1-bedroom Hollywood apartments crammed with immigrants. They aren't being rounded up because they aren't brown.

The Uber driver takes us to In and Out. We don't eat beef so we get fries. Blocky, congealed melted cheese. My daughter says, "Missouri's fries are better." When we get back and people ask her what she thought about LA, she dwells on the cheese. I look around cautiously. The bright lights of the restaurant highlight the crowd. They are pockets of natives, one foot between them, but in their own world.

My daughter complains that everyone is unfriendly. I explain that people in LA are just private. Eyes forward. Eyes glazed. Perhaps fires in their memory cloud their gaze. "Why don't the Uber drivers talk to us?" I tell her they won't unless you talk to them first. ChatGPT told me that.

We get off the plane. Everyone is rushing, there is food everywhere, and fried grease in the air. We are starving. We agree that no one in LA eats. This is familiar, St. Louis gray and rainy. We have emerged from the airlock of California dreams, like we escaped a sensory deprivation tank filled with blocky cheese. No Uber driver to take us home--just my husband grinning ready to talk.

"How was LA?" he asks. "Everyone is a robot, the cheese is congealed, and no one eats," my daughter tells him. I stare ahead, raindrops on the window, another pocket of time ready to begin.
